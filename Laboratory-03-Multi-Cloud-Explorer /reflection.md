# Mission Reflection

## 1. Which cloud platform impressed you the most?
Out of the three, AWS impressed me the most. What stood out was the sheer range of services — compute, storage, databases, networking, and security all under one platform — flexible enough for a solo developer or a large enterprise. Going through its tools made me want to actually get hands-on with it beyond just reading the documentation.

## 2. What similarities exist among AWS, Azure, and GCP?
AWS, Azure, and GCP all cover the same core ground: virtual servers, storage, databases, networking, security, identity management, and Kubernetes support. All three let organizations run applications, manage data, and scale in similar ways. Where they really differ is pricing, service naming, specific features, and which areas each provider chooses to specialize in.

## 3. How do business requirements influence cloud platform selection?
Business requirements are one of the biggest factors in choosing a cloud provider. Organizations have to weigh budget, security needs, performance, scalability, their existing software stack, and long-term goals. A company built around Microsoft tools would likely lean toward Azure, while one focused on data analytics or AI would lean toward GCP. The right platform depends on what the business is actually solving for.

## 4. What did you learn from acting as a Cloud Solutions Consultant?
This activity showed me that choosing a cloud platform isn't about picking whichever provider has the biggest name. A Cloud Solutions Consultant has to understand the client's goals, technical setup, budget, and future plans before recommending anything. Working through this lab helped me practice comparing services across providers and building a recommendation that fits a specific client instead of defaulting to the most popular option.

## 5. How has your GitHub Cloud Computing Portfolio improved after completing this mission?
My GitHub portfolio grew again with this third lab folder, and my Git workflow specifically got better. I still ended up with more commits than I probably needed, mostly from small mistakes along the way, but this time I started writing clearer, more descriptive commit messages instead of vague ones. If I need to go back and figure out what I changed and why, I can actually tell from the history now instead of guessing. Overall, the portfolio feels more structured than after other laboratory activities .
